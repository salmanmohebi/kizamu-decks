# From C1 to C2 · a study guide for sounding native

A companion to the `kizamu-english::C2` decks. It covers how to use the decks, the
methods that work at this level, the tools, and a weekly plan.

## 1. Why C1 learners plateau

At C1 you understand almost everything and can say almost anything. The gap to an
educated native speaker is now mostly **not knowledge, but choice and sound**:

| What gives a C1 speaker away | Example | What natives do |
|---|---|---|
| Wrong word partner (collocation) | *make a photo, high rain, do a mistake* | *take a photo, heavy rain, make a mistake* |
| Formal words in casual speech | *I will purchase it. Shall we commence?* | *I'll buy it. Should we get started?* |
| Written linkers in speech | *Moreover, … Furthermore, …* | *Plus, … On top of that, … And the thing is, …* |
| No hedging, too direct | *You are wrong.* | *I'm not sure that's quite right.* |
| No contractions | *I do not think it is ready.* | *I don't think it's ready.* |
| Translated structures | *since three years, discuss about, until now (= so far)* | *for three years, discuss, so far* |
| Flat rhythm and stress | every syllable equal | stress-timed rhythm, reduced function words |
| Rare "impressive" words | *That is a quotidian occurrence.* | *That happens all the time.* |

The decks target exactly these points. The last row matters: **a rare word in the wrong
place makes you sound less native, not more.** Every card says where an item fits.

## 2. How to use the decks

**Daily routine (20–30 min).** Study the parent deck `kizamu-english::C2`. New cards come
in order of importance and mix all categories. The preset gives 20 new cards a day;
about half are Meaning cards (new items) and half are Usage cards for items you met a
few days earlier.

**Meaning card.** Read the term in its sentence. Say what it means *and* when you would
use it (chat? email? paper?). Only then flip.

**Usage card.** This is where you build active use. Say the full sentence **aloud**
before you flip. Grade honestly: if you needed the hint, or you produced a weaker
synonym, press *Hard* or *Again*.

**Read every example aloud.** Listen once, then say it with the same rhythm. This is
mini-shadowing and costs 10 seconds per card.

**Already know it?** If a Meaning card is truly easy *and* you already use the item
actively, press *Easy*. If you would never need it, suspend it. Do not keep cards you
resent.

**Focus on one area.** Each category has its own subdecks (Set 01 = most useful). You
can also build a filtered deck by tag, for example `tag:theme::research-academia`,
`tag:register::informal` or `tag:importance::5`.

**Use the item within 24 hours.** One email, one sentence in a meeting, one message to
a friend. Use is what turns a card into vocabulary.

## 3. Methods that work at C1–C2

### Shadowing (10 minutes a day)

1. Take 1–2 minutes of natural American speech **with a transcript**: a podcast episode
   page, YouTube with subtitles, or a TED talk.
2. Listen once while reading.
3. Play it again and speak **at the same time** as the speaker, a fraction of a second
   behind. Copy rhythm, stress, pauses and melody, not only the words.
4. Record yourself on one passage per week and compare. Look for missing reductions
   (*gonna*, *'em*), wrong stress, and flat intonation.

### Sentence mining

When a native speaker says something you would not have said, write down the **whole
sentence**, not just the word. Collect them in a list (for example
`english/my-items.md`). The same pipeline can turn this list into full cards
with meaning, usage, examples and audio.

### Reading like a writer

Read a little every day from sources with excellent American prose (see §5). Once a
week, take one paragraph and:

- underline every collocation (*a sharp decline*, *raise concerns*);
- note how sentences start and how ideas are linked;
- rewrite the paragraph from memory 30 minutes later, then compare it with the original.
  The differences are exactly your next items to learn.

### Writing with feedback

Write something short every week: a summary of a paper, an email, a paragraph of
opinion. Then ask an editor (a colleague, a tutor or an LLM) for **naturalness**
feedback, not grammar. A useful prompt:

> Rewrite this as an educated American researcher would write it. Keep my meaning. Then
> list every change in a table: my version, your version, and why yours sounds more
> natural (collocation, register, rhythm, idiom). Do not change things that are already
> natural.

Add the best changes to your sentence-mining list.

### Speaking practice

- **Two-minute monologue.** Pick three items from today's cards and talk about any topic
  for two minutes while using all three. Record it.
- **Self-talk.** Describe what you are doing in English while cooking or walking.
- **Tutor sessions** (italki, Preply or a local tutor): ask the tutor to interrupt you
  *only* for things that sound unnatural, and to give the native version.
- In meetings, try one new discourse marker a week (*That said*, *If anything*,
  *To be fair*) until it comes out without effort.

### Pronunciation: the American features that matter most

1. **Stress-timed rhythm.** Content words are long and stressed; function words
   (*to, for, and, of, a, the, can*) are short with a schwa /ə/. *I want to go* →
   "I wanna GO".
2. **Flap T.** Between vowels, *t* sounds like a quick *d*: *water, better, a lot of,
   get it*.
3. **Reduced and dropped T.** *Button, mountain* (glottal stop); *twenty, internet*
   (nasal flap).
4. **Linking.** Final consonant joins the next vowel: *turn it off* → "tur-ni-toff".
5. **Can vs. can't.** *Can* is usually reduced (/kən/); *can't* keeps a full vowel and a
   stop: /kænʔ/.
6. **Word stress.** Wrong stress hurts understanding more than a wrong vowel. Check every
   new long word with an audio dictionary.
7. **Intonation.** Statements fall at the end; a rise often sounds unsure.

## 4. Tools

| Tool | What it is for |
|---|---|
| **YouGlish** (youglish.com) | Hear any word or phrase in hundreds of real YouTube clips; filter to US English |
| **COCA** (english-corpora.org/coca) | Corpus of Contemporary American English: check if and how natives use a phrase |
| **SkELL** (skell.sketchengine.eu) | Free, simple corpus tool: example sentences and word partners for any word |
| **Google Books Ngram Viewer** | Compare how common two phrasings are, and whether a phrase is dated |
| **Netspeak** or **Ludwig** | Check which word fits a gap: `make ? decision` |
| **Oxford Collocations Dictionary** (also online as ozdic.com) | Word partners by meaning |
| **Merriam-Webster** and **Cambridge Dictionary (US)** | American pronunciation with audio; usage notes |
| **Language Reactor** (browser extension) | Dual subtitles and sentence saving for Netflix and YouTube |
| **Rachel's English** (YouTube) | American pronunciation, connected speech and rhythm |

Rule of thumb: **do not trust a thesaurus** for production. A synonym list gives words
with the wrong connotation or collocation. Check a candidate in YouGlish, COCA or SkELL
before you use it.

## 5. What to read, watch and listen to (American English)

**Podcasts — clear, educated speech:** *The Daily* (NYT), *Planet Money*, *Hidden
Brain*, *Freakonomics Radio*, *Fresh Air*, *Radiolab*, *This American Life*.
**Casual, fast conversation:** *Conan O'Brien Needs a Friend*, *SmartLess*.
**Tech and research:** *Acquired*, *Hard Fork*, *Lex Fridman Podcast* (long interviews).

**TV — everyday and workplace American English:** *The Office (US)*, *Parks and
Recreation*, *Silicon Valley*, *Succession* (business language), *Abbott Elementary*,
*The Bear* (fast, informal). Watch with English subtitles; turn them off on a second
viewing.

**Reading:** *The Atlantic*, *The New Yorker*, *Wired*, *ProPublica*; for writing
craft, Steven Pinker's *The Sense of Style* and William Zinsser's *On Writing Well*.

**YouTube:** *Vox*, *Veritasium*, *Johnny Harris*, *Rachel's English*.

| Weekly | Two-minute recorded monologue with three target items | 10 min |
| Weekly | One tutor or conversation session (optional) | 30–60 min |
| Monthly | Compare a new recording with last month's | 15 min |

## 7. Ten quick fixes for European speakers

1. *Actual* ≠ *aktuell*. *Actually* = "in fact", not "currently".
2. *Eventually* = "in the end", not "possibly" (*eventuell* = *possibly*).
3. *Since* + point in time (*since 2019*); *for* + period (*for three years*).
4. *So far* (until now, and it continues) vs. *until now* (it just changed).
5. *Discuss* a topic, *explain* something *to* someone, *emphasize* a point — no extra
   preposition.
6. *Make* a decision / mistake / point; *take* a photo / a break / a look; *do* research.
7. *Become* = *werden*, never *bekommen* (*bekommen* = *get, receive*): *I got an email*,
   not *I became an email*.
8. Use *Ms.* / first names in US emails; *Dear Sir or Madam* sounds old-fashioned.
9. Hedge requests: *Could you take a look when you have a minute?* instead of
   *Please check this.*
10. In speech, prefer short Germanic verbs (*find out, set up, go over*) to Latin ones
    (*ascertain, establish, review*); keep the Latin ones for writing.
